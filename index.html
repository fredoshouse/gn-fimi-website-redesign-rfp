01 · Why we're here

You have the evidence.
The site is the bottleneck.

FIMI's research makes one of the strongest health economics cases in the country. It just isn't landing where it needs to. The people who can act on this work, the health system executive and the legislative staffer, arrive at a site built for a different reader entirely.

We want to build the version of this site where the argument makes itself.

Why this matters
Behind every dataset on this site is a meal on someone's table, a diagnosis that went differently, a family that got more time.

That's the story the numbers are carrying. The new site has to let people feel it, not just file it.

02 · The audit

What we found
at the front door.

01
Four hero cards, one identical image
The homepage rotates four hero panels, About, Projects, Updates, and Partners, and all four load the exact same photo file. The page is telling a first-time visitor that everything matters equally, and it's saying it with identical art. Nothing signals where a healthcare leader or a policymaker should go.
02
Your strongest content is buried
The FIM Facts stats, 85% of healthcare spending, the $24B figure, the 49% hospitalization reduction, are the most persuasive things on the entire site. They sit near the bottom of the homepage as static text, with the full fact sheet locked inside a PDF download. That's your lead argument, hidden.
03
Eight nav items, no orientation, one dead link
The top nav carries eight flat items with no hierarchy and no guidance. "Partners" goes nowhere on its own, it only opens a dropdown. And the nav isn't even consistent. The submenu items change depending on which page you're on. The Federal Nutrition Advisory Coalition appears in the Partners menu on some pages and vanishes on others.
04
Your best tool lives on a different site
The FIM Toolkit, probably the single most actionable asset for a clinician or health system, sits on a separate subdomain with no integrated presence. Your most conversion-ready resource is a departure point, not a destination.
02 · The audit

What we found
underneath.

05
Project pages speak grant, not decision
Take your Medically Tailored Meal Interventions page. The headline is the grant title. The body opens with study design, then Aim One through Aim Four in nested academic lists, some with visibly broken numbering. Publications appear as raw citations. The only way to engage is a mailto link to one staff member's personal email. There isn't a single image, chart, or plain-language takeaway on the page. A hospital COO closes that tab in fifteen seconds.
06
A taxonomy exists, but it speaks researcher
The projects listing has real filters, which tells us someone on your team cared about this. But the categories are "Intervention Study / Evaluation" and "Cardiometabolic Disease," and every card wears a long string of dot-separated tags that mean nothing to your two primary audiences. One listing card even leaks raw unformatted body text straight into the grid. The bones are there. The audience logic isn't.
07
Accessibility has a real problem, today
The site's viewport settings disable pinch-to-zoom on mobile. That's not a style choice, it's a barrier for low-vision visitors, and it conflicts with WCAG 2.1 AA, the exact standard your RFP requires. It should be fixed no matter who wins this work.
08
Technical debt is quietly taxing you
Images serve from two domains at once, the public site and the raw Pantheon URL. At least one key page declares an http canonical instead of https. Small things individually. Together they cost performance, dilute search authority, and suggest nobody's minding the store between your site and Google.

And to be clear, none of this is a knock on the 2023 build. It did its job for a brand new institute, and someone on your team clearly cared about it. The Institute simply outgrew it. That's a good problem, and it's the reason this project exists.

02 · The audit

It's a repository.
It needs to be an argument.

A repository organizes what exists. An argument moves someone toward a decision.

Everything on your current site is accurate. Almost none of it is arranged to persuade. There's no synthesis layer, no through-line from a clinical trial result to "here's why your health system should pilot this in Q1," no version of the research written for someone who will never read a methods section.

That gap is the whole project.

02 · The audit

The new site has four jobs.
In this order.

01
Establish
Before a visitor scrolls, they should know FIMI is the source on food is medicine, not one more voice in the field. First screen. No exceptions.
02
Translate
Every project, publication, and finding gets a plain-language read written for a decision-maker. The science stays rigorous. The framing stops assuming a PhD.
03
Connect
A visitor should never hit a dead end. Every page opens into related evidence, related people, and related tools, powered by taxonomy rather than maintained by hand.
04
Convert
Each audience gets a specific next step in their own language. "Talk to us about piloting this." "Request a policy briefing." Not one shared contact form for everyone.
03 · Our approach

Two readers.
One site.

Your RFP names two primary audiences, and most redesigns will answer that with two landing pages and call it segmentation. We think that fails, because people don't enter through the front door. They land on a project page from a Google search or a colleague's forwarded link.

So the audience split doesn't live in the navigation. It lives inside the page templates.

Every substantive page carries a dual read: what this means if you run a health system, and what this means if you write policy. Same evidence, two translations, side by side. A visitor self-selects in one glance, on whatever page a search engine dropped them.

If you run a health system
Cost, utilization, feasibility.
  • What this evidence means for your cost curve and readmission rates
  • What implementing it actually took: setting, population, duration, partners
  • A direct path to pilot or scale with FIMI's support
If you write policy
Evidence, precedent, levers.
  • Eligible population and projected budget impact, ready to cite
  • Where this evidence has already shaped legislation, regulation, or guidance
  • A direct path to a briefing or technical assistance
03 · Our approach

Two clicks.
Every time.

Your brief defines the ideal journey for each audience. Here's how the architecture delivers it, from any entry point, not just the homepage.

The healthcare leader
Lands, understands, acts.
  • Lands. Poor diet is driving costs and outcomes, and food is medicine is a proven, scalable response. That's the first screen.
  • Click one. The Impact hub, filtered to cost and utilization outcomes, in executive language.
  • Click two. A project page with real-world implementation detail, the tools to act, and relevant partner examples.
  • Acts. Requests a conversation about piloting or scaling a FIM program.
The policymaker
Lands, trusts, cites.
  • Lands. FIMI is the credible, nonpartisan academic authority on food is medicine. Unmistakable, immediately.

  • Click two. A specific brief, testimony, or public comment, with the underlying evidence one tap away.
  • Acts. Requests a briefing, accesses tools, or engages FIMI for technical assistance.

04 · Design thinking

One required field
changes everything.

Every project gets a mandatory plain-language summary, capped at roughly 40 words, written for someone with zero nutrition science background. The CMS won't let a project publish without it.

That single constraint does more for your two primary audiences than any visual decision on any page. It forces the translation to happen once, at the source, instead of being reinvented on every collection page, search result, and related-content card.

It's small to build and real to commit to. We'd rather name that now than surprise you with it in October.

05 · The build

WordPress.
And it isn't close.

You asked vendors to make a case rather than state a preference. Here's ours, and it starts with something we confirmed in the audit: your current site already runs WordPress on the Tufts Pantheon account.

A
Re-platforming is your biggest avoidable risk
Choosing Drupal means a full platform migration stacked on top of a full redesign, on a five-month runway, through the December holidays. Nothing in your requirements justifies taking that on.
B
Your own primary consideration decides it
You said non-technical staff self-sufficiency matters most. Your team already knows the WordPress admin. Drupal means redesigning the site and retraining every editor in the same quarter. WordPress means launch day feels familiar, just dramatically better.
C
The taxonomy requirement doesn't need Drupal
The usual argument for Drupal is complex content modeling. Custom post types with shared taxonomies and bidirectional relationship fields handle all eight of your content types cleanly. We spell out how, two slides from now.
D
Pantheon stays exactly as Tufts knows it
Same dev, test, and live workflow. Multidev branches for review. We work inside that structure from day one and hand it back to Tufts the way we found it, minus the split-domain asset issue, which we fix.

05 · The build

A locked block library.
Not a page builder.

We build on the native WordPress block editor with a custom library of locked, brand-compliant blocks. Your team assembles pages from approved components. They can't accidentally break a layout, and they never touch code.

We deliberately avoid third-party page builders. They create more ways to break a design than they solve, they slow pages down, and they leave you holding a licensing dependency after we're gone.

What day to day looks like
Fill a form. Tag. Publish.
  • Adding a publication: paste the citation, write the summary, tag the related project and team members, publish
  • Every connected page updates itself. The project page, the author profiles, the collection filters, all of it
  • Homepage stats, spotlight, and news modules editable as simple fields, no layout risk
Guardrails built in
Safe by default.
  • Locked blocks enforce Tufts brand rules automatically: colors, type, spacing
  • The 40-word plain-language summary is a required field, enforced at publish
  • Alt text prompts and heading-order checks keep accessibility from drifting after launch

07 · Timeline

Is January realistic?
Yes, with three conditions.

You asked for a straight answer, so here it is. January 2027 is achievable and it is not generous. Three risks decide it, and each one has a plan.

01
Serial approvals
Two feedback rounds per milestone plus a university brand review is a chain, and a chain moves at the speed of its slowest link. Our mitigation: overlapping phases as shown, Tufts marketing pulled into the design strategy gate before mockups exist, and the seven-day feedback window written into the contract rather than assumed.
02
Content decisions
Roughly 200 publications and 30 projects need retain-or-retire calls, and every project needs a new plain-language summary. Nothing on the critical path is more likely to slip. Our mitigation: the audit lands week four, decisions lock by September 30, and student support is scoped against the mechanical work so your staff spends judgment, not hours.
03
December
Two weeks are effectively gone and pretending otherwise is how launches die. We plan for it. Development completes December 18, migration wraps December 19, and QA opens January 4 with a real buffer instead of a scramble.

One honest caveat. If contract execution slips past August 31, the math changes, and we'd recommend an early February launch over a compressed QA window. We'd tell you that in September, not at Christmas.
